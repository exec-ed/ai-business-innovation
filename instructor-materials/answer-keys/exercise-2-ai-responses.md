**Purpose:** Show instructors what AI typically generates so you can calibrate student experiences

**Use this to:**

## Round 1: Risk Deep-Dive Sample Responses

### Example 1: Retail Industry

**Student Prompt:**
> "You are a strategic retail consultant. Our team identified these AI threats: (1) Online-first competitors with AI-powered dynamic pricing, (2) Amazon entering our category with private label, (3) Direct-to-consumer brands with AI customer acquisition. What are we missing or underestimating?"

**Typical AI Response:**

> "You've identified competitive threats, but here are risks you're likely missing or underestimating:
> 
> **What you're missing:**
> 
> 1. **Platform aggregators using AI for discovery:** Google Shopping, Pinterest, TikTok using AI to directly compete with you for product discovery, bypassing your website entirely. Early warning sign: declining organic traffic.
> 
> 2. **Vertical integration by suppliers:** Your suppliers using AI to go direct-to-consumer, cutting you out. They have product expertise + AI can solve their distribution/marketing gap.
> 
> 3. **Gen Z expectations shaped by AI:** Customers under 25 expect AI-powered personalization, virtual try-on, instant answers. Without this, you're "old-fashioned" regardless of price/product.
> 
> **What you're underestimating:**
> 
> 1. **Second-order effect of dynamic pricing:** It's not just about pricing—competitors using AI pricing are also collecting real-time price elasticity data, which then improves their product development, merchandising, and inventory. The data flywheel effect means they get smarter faster.
> 
> 2. **Speed of capability building:** You noted "Amazon entering your category"—by the time you see this threat, they've already been building AI capabilities for 12-18 months. The visible threat is lagging indicator.
> 
> 3. **Customer data devaluation:** As competitors build AI-powered customer understanding, YOUR customer data becomes less valuable over time (you're learning slowly, they're learning fast). This is an accelerating gap.
> 
> **Most likely to be existential:** #3 (Gen Z expectations). If an entire generation finds your shopping experience "dated," no amount of price competition saves you. This is a 3-5 year death spiral.
> 
> **Early warning signs:**
> - Customer age distribution shifting older (younger customers going elsewhere)
> - Session times decreasing (finding products faster elsewhere)
> - "How do I..." support tickets increasing (UX falling behind expectations)

**What students learn:**
- AI connects dots they didn't (dynamic pricing → data flywheel → product development)
- AI brings adjacent industry examples (platform aggregators)
- AI identifies second-order effects (Gen Z expectations shift)

### Example 2: Financial Services

**Student Prompt:**
> "You are a strategic banking consultant. We identified: (1) Fintech startups with AI-powered underwriting, (2) Big tech entering payments (Apple, Google), (3) Robo-advisors replacing human advisors. What are we missing?"

**Typical AI Response:**

> **What you're missing:**
> 
> 1. **Embedded finance powered by AI:** Your customers getting financial services WITHOUT visiting you—embedded in retail checkouts, employer benefits, social media. AI makes embedding seamless. You become invisible.
> 
> 2. **Regulatory arbitrage by AI-native competitors:** Startups architected from day-one for AI compliance (explainability, fairness testing). You're retrofitting AI onto legacy systems with compliance debt.
> 
> 3. **Talent drain to tech companies:** Your best people leave for firms "doing real AI." This accelerates competitive gap.
> 
> **What you're underestimating:**
> 
> 1. **Data moat is defensive, not offensive:** Big tech already has more data than you. Your customer data advantage is eroding, not growing.
> 
> 2. **Customer trust shift:** Younger customers trust tech brands (Apple, Google) MORE than traditional banks. AI alone won't fix trust deficit.
> 
> **Most existential:** Embedded finance. If customers never visit your app/branch because they bank where they shop/work, you lose pricing power and customer relationship.

**What students learn:**
- AI reveals trends from other industries (embedded finance from retail)
- AI identifies talent/organizational risks (not just competitive product risks)
- AI challenges assumptions about competitive advantages ("data moat")

### Example 3: Manufacturing

**Student Prompt:**
> "You are a manufacturing strategy consultant. Threats we see: (1) Predictive maintenance AI reducing downtime, (2) AI quality control replacing inspectors, (3) Automated supply chain optimization. What are we missing?"

**Typical AI Response:**

> **What you're missing:**
> 
> 1. **AI-designed products:** Generative design AI creating products humans couldn't design—lighter, stronger, cheaper. Competitors' products become functionally superior, not just cheaper.
> 
> 2. **Micro-factories with AI orchestration:** Small, distributed, AI-coordinated production competing with your centralized facilities. Lower capital costs, faster customization.
> 
> 3. **Supplier AI sophistication:** Your suppliers using AI for yield optimization means they have pricing power over you (they know your willingness-to-pay better than you know their costs).
> 
> **What you're underestimating:**
> 
> 1. **Compound effect of small improvements:** Each AI optimization (maintenance, quality, supply chain) is only 3-5% improvement. But competitors doing ALL of them = 15-20% cost advantage. That's existential.
> 
> 2. **Skilled labor scarcity:** As AI automates routine tasks, demand spikes for "AI trainers" and "automation supervisors." Labor market shift makes hiring harder, not easier.

**What students learn:**
- AI identifies non-obvious threats (generative design, micro-factories)
- AI shows how small improvements compound
- AI considers talent/labor market effects

## Round 2: Reverse Prompting Sample Responses

### Example Dialogue 1: Healthcare

**Student Prompt:**
> "You are an expert on AI in healthcare. Ask me questions to help discover opportunities. Industry: Hospital system. Current AI maturity: Level 2. Ask one question at a time."

**AI Question 1:**
> "What's the most time-consuming administrative task that takes your clinical staff away from patient care?"

**Student Answer:**
> "Probably documentation—nurses spend 2-3 hours per shift on notes."

**AI Question 2:**
> "Of those 2-3 hours, what percentage is duplicative information already captured elsewhere in your system?"

**Student Answer:**
> "Hmm, maybe 40-50%? We enter patient vitals in the monitor, then again in the EHR, then summarize in notes..."

**AI Question 3:**
> "If AI could auto-populate those duplicative fields from existing data sources, how would your nurses use the freed-up time?"

**Student Answer:**
> "More patient interaction, which would improve satisfaction and probably catch issues earlier... oh wait, this could improve clinical outcomes AND operational efficiency!"

**AI Question 4:**
> "What's preventing you from implementing this AI-powered documentation assistant today?"

**Student Answer:**
> "Data integration—our systems don't talk to each other well. And probably regulatory concerns about AI in clinical documentation."

**AI Question 5:**
> "Between those two barriers, which is easier to address in the next 6-12 months?"

**Student Answer:**
> "Data integration, definitely. Regulatory concerns are thornier... so we should start with non-clinical documentation first, build confidence, then expand."

**AI Synthesis:**
> "Based on your answers, I see an opportunity: Start with AI-assisted documentation for administrative (not clinical) tasks where regulatory barriers are lower. This gives quick wins (time savings), builds technical capability (data integration), and creates proof points for eventual clinical documentation AI. The path forward: 6-month pilot on admin documentation while you tackle data integration, then scale to clinical after proving value and addressing regulatory concerns."

**What instructor should observe:**
- AI's questions revealed the opportunity hiding in their complaint (documentation burden)
- Questions led them to discover the blocker (data integration, not AI)
- Questions helped prioritize (admin first, clinical later)
- Student had "aha!" at question 3 (changed perspective)

### Example Dialogue 2: Retail

**AI Question 1:**
> "What customer behavior do you wish you could predict but currently can't?"

**Student Answer:**
> "Which customers are about to churn—stop shopping with us and go to competitors."

**AI Question 2:**
> "What early signals of churn do you currently track, if any?"

**Student Answer:**
> "We track purchase frequency. If someone who usually shops monthly hasn't bought in 60 days, we might send a coupon."

**AI Question 3:**
> "That's reactive—you've already lost them for 60 days. What leading indicators might predict churn BEFORE it happens?"

**Student Answer:**
> "Hmm... maybe engagement metrics? Email open rates, website visits, time between purchases shortening then stopping, cart abandonment increasing..."

**AI Question 4:**
> "If you could predict churn 30 days in advance with 80% accuracy, what would you do differently than your current 'send coupon' approach?"

**Student Answer:**
> "We'd intervene earlier, personalize the intervention based on WHY they're churning, maybe VIP treatment for high-value customers... this is way more strategic than just coupons."

**AI Question 5:**
> "Do you currently collect and integrate all those leading indicators you mentioned, or would this require data infrastructure work first?"

**Student Answer:**
> "Oh. We collect them separately but they're not integrated. Email system separate from website analytics separate from transaction data. We'd need to connect these first."

**What instructor should observe:**
- Questions led from reactive (60-day lag) to proactive (30-day prediction)
- Student discovered the opportunity themselves (AI asked questions, didn't give answers)
- Final question revealed blocker (data integration needed first)
- This is "reverse prompting" working perfectly

## Round 3: AI Debate Sample

### Example: "Invest Now vs. Wait for Data Readiness"

**Context:** Mid-sized company, Level 2-3 data maturity, $2M budget, competitive pressure

**AI Optimist (ChatGPT) Opening:**
> "We must invest in AI NOW despite data limitations. Here's why: (1) Waiting for 'perfect' data means competitors pull 18 months ahead while we optimize spreadsheets. (2) Building AI creates the forcing function to fix data—you'll never prioritize data infrastructure without a compelling use case. (3) First-mover advantages in AI are real—early data collection creates compounding returns. Start with imperfect data, improve as you go. Paralysis costs more than imperfect action."

**AI Pragmatist (Claude) Opening:**
> "Rushing into AI with poor data foundations is how 85% of AI projects fail. Here's reality: (1) 'Garbage in, garbage out' isn't just a saying—it's a $2M lesson in wasted investment. (2) You can't fix data 'as you go'—you'll spend 80% of project time on data cleaning instead of AI. (3) Better approach: Invest 40% in data infrastructure NOW, then do AI in Year 2 with solid foundation. Patient money beats rushed money."

**Optimist Counter:**
> "You're assuming we have time to be patient. The competitive landscape says we don't. Waiting 12 months for 'perfect' data means: (1) Competitors capture customer relationships we'll never win back. (2) Our best talent leaves for companies 'doing real AI.' (3) Board loses confidence in leadership. We can pilot AI on our BEST data sources (customer transactions are clean) while fixing other sources in parallel."

**Pragmatist Counter:**
> "Pilot approach makes sense—but only if you're honest about limitations. (1) Don't promise transformational results from a data-constrained pilot. (2) Don't let 'pilot success' pressure you into scaling before data is ready. (3) The real question: Will you actually invest in data infrastructure in parallel, or will political pressure push you to scale the imperfect pilot? History suggests the latter."

**Optimist Final:**
> "You're right to be cautious about scaling prematurely. Here's the synthesis: (1) Pilot NOW on highest-quality data sources. (2) Simultaneously invest in data infrastructure (not sequentially). (3) Define clear 'data readiness gates' for scaling. This balances urgency with prudence."

**Pragmatist Final:**
> "Agreed on simultaneous approach, with caveat: Budget MUST reflect both—60% AI pilot, 40% data infrastructure. Not 90/10. And leadership must commit to NOT scaling if data gates aren't met, even if pilot shows promise. That's discipline most organizations lack."

**What instructor should observe:**
- Debate clarifies the actual decision (not "invest or wait" but "invest with discipline")
- Optimist and Pragmatist both make valid points (no clear "winner")
- Synthesis emerges: Pilot + data infrastructure in parallel + clear gates
- This mirrors real executive debates (both perspectives exist on teams)

**Facilitation after debate:**
"Show of hands: Who was more convinced by Optimist?" [Some hands]  
"Who by Pragmatist?" [Some hands]

"The synthesis is usually: Do BOTH. Pilot on best data while fixing infrastructure. But commit to NOT scaling until data is ready. That's strategic discipline."

## What "Good" AI Engagement Looks Like

### Strong Table Engagement

**You'll hear:**
- "Wait, that's a really good point we didn't consider"
- "Let me ask AI a follow-up question about that"
- "Do we actually have that data AI is assuming we have?"
- Debate about whether AI's suggestion is applicable
- Someone captures AI insight on sticky note

**You'll see:**
- Animated discussion
- Multiple people reading AI output
- Follow-up prompts being typed
- Integration of AI insights with human brainstorm

### Weak Table Engagement

**You'll hear:**
- Silence while reading AI response
- "Yeah, that's obvious" (dismissive)
- "AI doesn't understand our business" (defensive)
- Moving on without discussing

**You'll see:**
- One person reading AI output alone
- No follow-up prompts
- AI insights not captured
- Quick moving to next round without processing

**How to fix:**
"Stop. Read AI's response aloud. What's one thing AI said that you didn't have on your sticky notes? Even if it's not perfect, what's the kernel of insight?"

## Prompt Quality Indicators

### Good Prompts (will get useful AI responses)

**Characteristics:**
- ✅ Specific industry context ("mid-sized retail, Western US, $500M revenue")
- ✅ Clear role for AI ("You are a strategic consultant specializing in...")
- ✅ Concrete inputs ("Here are our current threats: [LIST]")
- ✅ Specific questions ("What are we missing?")
- ✅ Structure for output ("Rank by likelihood × impact")

**Example:**
> "You are a strategic consultant for regional retail chains. We're a $500M omnichannel retailer facing these AI threats: [LIST]. What threats from adjacent industries should we worry about? What second-order effects are we missing? Rank by impact."

**You'll see:** Specific, industry-grounded, actionable AI responses

### Poor Prompts (will get generic AI responses)

**Characteristics:**
- ❌ Vague context ("We're in business")
- ❌ No role for AI
- ❌ Generic question ("Tell me about AI threats")
- ❌ No structure for output

**Example:**
> "What AI threats should we worry about?"

**You'll see:** Generic, broad, not actionable AI responses like "AI is disrupting all industries. Automation threatens jobs. Data privacy is a concern."

**How to fix in real-time:**
"That response was too generic because your prompt lacked context. Try adding: your industry, your specific situation, what you've already considered. Let me show you..." [Model good prompt]

## Common Student Discoveries

### Discovery 1: "AI identified a threat from an adjacent industry we never considered"

**Example:**
- Banking students: "AI said fintech API platforms could make us 'invisible infrastructure'"
- Retail students: "AI mentioned social commerce (TikTok shop) bypassing our website"
- Manufacturing: "AI suggested generative design creating superior products"

**Why this happens:** AI has breadth across industries humans don't

**Facilitation:**
"That's AI's strength—it can pull examples from industries you don't track. You brought depth (knowing your specific business), AI brought breadth (knowing many industries). Together = better than either alone."

### Discovery 2: "Reverse prompting revealed an opportunity we've been sitting on"

**Example:**
Student: "We've been doing [X] manually for 10 years"  
AI: "Why manually?"  
Student: "We just... always have. I guess we could automate it with AI. We never questioned it."

**Why this happens:** AI's questions challenge assumptions we don't know we have

**Facilitation:**
"This is the power of reverse prompting. Sometimes the question is more valuable than the answer. AI made you question something you took for granted."

### Discovery 3: "The AI debate clarified what we should actually do"

**Example:**
Student: "We were stuck between 'invest aggressively' and 'wait for readiness.' The debate showed us: do BOTH—pilot on best data while building infrastructure."

**Why this happens:** Debates force exploration of multiple perspectives, synthesis emerges

**Facilitation:**
"Right—it's rarely binary. Usually the answer is 'do X in short term, prepare for Y in parallel.' The debate helps you see that."

### Discovery 4: "AI's accuracy varies wildly"

**Example:**
Student: "AI said our industry is [X] but that's not true for us specifically."

**Why this happens:** AI generalizes, doesn't know your specific context

**Facilitation:**
"Perfect observation. This is why you can't accept AI output blindly. AI provides hypotheses, YOU validate with your expertise. That's the human-AI collaboration model."

## Red Flags to Watch For

### Red Flag 1: "AI said it, so it must be true"

**Symptom:** Students accept AI output without critical evaluation

**Fix:**
"Wait—is that actually true for YOUR business? Or is AI generalizing?"

**Teaching moment:**
"AI can be confidently wrong. Your job is to evaluate AI's suggestions critically. Take what's useful, discard what's not applicable."

### Red Flag 2: "AI is useless, it doesn't understand our business"

**Symptom:** Dismissive attitude, no engagement with AI output

**Fix:**
"You're right, AI doesn't know your specific business. But did it identify ANYTHING worth considering? One insight has value."

**Then probe:**
"Why didn't AI understand your business? What context was missing from your prompt?"

**Teaching moment:**
"Better prompts = better results. Try being more specific about your situation."

### Red Flag 3: "Let's just ask AI for the answer"

**Symptom:** Over-reliance, trying to skip human thinking

**Fix:**
"AI helps you THINK, frameworks help you DECIDE. We're not outsourcing judgment to AI.

Use AI for: Breadth, alternatives, challenging assumptions.  
Use YOUR expertise for: Context, judgment, final decision."

**Teaching moment:**
"This afternoon in Dragon's Den, you'll use frameworks to decide which AI to fund. AI can help analyze, but YOU decide. That's the right balance."

### Red Flag 4: "This is taking too long"

**Symptom:** Impatience with the process, rushing through prompts

**Acknowledge:**
"I know this feels slow. You're learning a new skill—prompt engineering.

In 2 months when you've done this 10 times, you'll be fast. Today, we're building muscle memory."

**Reality check:**
If tables are genuinely struggling (13+ minutes into Round 1, still no AI response), time-box it:
"Okay, let's move on. I'll show an example later."

## Timing Flexibility

### If Ahead of Schedule (Phase 2 done in 20 min)

**Add:** 
- Round 4: "AI as Devil's Advocate" (10 min)
  - Pick your best opportunity
  - Ask AI to argue against it
  - Strengthens your thinking

**Or:**
- Deeper discussion in Phase 3 (extend to 20 min)
- More tables share in presentations (all instead of 3)

### If Behind Schedule (Phase 2 taking 40 min)

**Cut:**
- Skip AI Debate (save 10 min)
  - Or YOU present pre-prepared debate example (save 5 min)
- Shorten Phase 3 to 10 min (quick shareout, skip deep discussion)

**Minimum viable:**
- Phase 1: Human brainstorm (15 min)
- Phase 2: Risk Deep-Dive only (10 min) - skip reverse prompting and debate
- Phase 3: Quick shareout (5 min)
- **Total:** 30 minutes (if severely behind)
