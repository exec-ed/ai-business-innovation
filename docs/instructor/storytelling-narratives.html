What Makes AI Different (Stories)

Story: “AI is a Confident Liar”

When to use: Explaining why AI is probabilistic (9:15-9:45 AM section)

The narrative: > “The biggest flaw of AI is that it hallucinates—it confidently invents plausible-sounding but completely false information. This isn’t a bug to be fixed; it’s a core feature of how Large Language Models work. > > AI is a pattern predictor, not a truth machine. It predicts the next most likely word, optimizing for fluency, not accuracy. Think of it as an extremely articulate improviser who’s read everything on the internet but can’t tell you when it’s uncertain or doesn’t know something. > > In specialized domains, hallucination rates can exceed 30%. That means one-third of the time, AI is confidently wrong. You cannot automate a critical business process with a tool that’s wrong a third of the time.”

Teaching point: “This is why we need the ‘human-in-the-loop’ principle. AI provides recommendations, humans verify and decide. Never give AI final authority on high-stakes decisions.”

When students say “But AI is getting better”: “Yes, and a 95% accurate AI is amazing. But if that 5% failure rate is on medical diagnoses or loan approvals, 5% is catastrophic. Always match AI accuracy to your risk tolerance.”

Story: “The 95% Implementation Failure Rate”

When to use: Motivating why AI-specific evaluation matters (9:15 AM intro)

The narrative: > “MIT research found that 95% of integrated AI pilots in businesses fail to generate measurable profit and loss impact. Only 5% extract significant value. > > Why such a high failure rate? Not because the AI doesn’t work technically. Because organisations aren’t ready: > - Data is messy, fragmented, contradictory > - Staff must manually check everything AI produces > - The productivity paradox: AI creates MORE work (verification) instead of saving time > - 55% of companies regret replacing people with AI for this reason”

Teaching point: “This is why we emphasize data readiness, change management, and human-in-loop processes. Technical AI success ≠ business AI success. Our frameworks address the 95% failure causes.”

Follow-up: “What’s your organization’s AI success rate so far? If it’s low, it’s not your fault—most organisations are in the same boat. Our goal today: Move you into the 5% that succeed.”

Story: “Amazon’s Organisational Amnesia”

When to use: Explaining why data readiness matters (11:00 AM Data Pyramid section)

The narrative: > “At Amazon, a product manager shared this: Big tech companies are fundamentally chaotic. ‘Two-pizza teams’ create speed but also massive fragmentation. Thousands of teams duplicating work, using conflicting definitions, making decisions in silos. > > The biggest cost isn’t technology—it’s wasted human hours. People spending days navigating bureaucracy, finding the right information, resolving conflicts between teams. > > High turnover means ‘organisational amnesia’—the ‘why’ behind decisions is constantly lost. Teams repeat mistakes from 3 years ago because the knowledge isn’t stored accessibly. Millions of dollars burned rediscovering what was already learned. > > AI was supposed to be the ‘corporate historian’ connecting these dots. But feeding messy, contradictory data into AI produces confident but wrong answers. The AI can’t tell the difference between your current strategy and a deprecated document from 2019.”

Teaching point: “This is why data readiness is criterion #1. Before you can do smart AI, you need smart data. Level 2 data maturity = fragmented, siloed sources. You cannot do Level 5 AI (autonomous decisions) on Level 2 data. You will fail.”

Real implication: “When you score data readiness in Exercise 1, be brutally honest. ‘We have data in SAP’ doesn’t mean data is AI-ready. Is it cleaned? Integrated? Consistent? Unbiased? If not, budget 40% of your AI investment for data infrastructure FIRST.”

Data Readiness Reality

Story: “The Garbage In, Garbage Out Principle”

When to use: Exercise 1 debrief (10:30 AM) or AI Investment Model section (1:15 PM)

The narrative: > “The promise of AI: Feed it your corporate data, it connects the dots, becomes your intelligent assistant. > > The reality: Corporate data is a disaster. It’s fragmented across systems, definitions vary by department, documentation is outdated, and the ‘why’ behind decisions is lost when employees leave. > > What happens when you feed garbage data into AI? The AI confidently produces garbage outputs—but polished, professional-sounding garbage. It synthesizes your fragmented data into coherent-sounding answers that are fundamentally wrong. > > Example: You ask AI ‘What’s our pricing strategy?’ It finds: > - 2024 strategy doc: ‘Premium positioning’ > - 2022 email thread: ‘Match competitors’ > - 2019 presentation: ‘Cost-plus pricing’ > > AI synthesizes: ‘Your pricing strategy is premium cost-plus matching competitors.’ Sounds reasonable. Completely useless.”

Teaching point: “This is why data readiness gates AI investment. If your data readiness score is <7, STOP. Fund data infrastructure first. Otherwise you’re building a skyscraper on quicksand.”

Story: “Why Enterprise AI Fails But Personal AI Works”

When to use: When students are skeptical about enterprise AI (11:30 AM Exercise 2)

The narrative: > “Paradox: ChatGPT works great for personal use. But enterprise AI implementations fail 95% of the time. Why? > > Personal AI: You curate your inputs. You provide context. You verify outputs. Small scope, high control. > > Enterprise AI: Fed 20 years of messy corporate data. Conflicting sources. No curation. Expected to ‘figure it out.’ > > The solution that works: Personal RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation). Instead of enterprise-wide AI on all corporate data, build personal AI grounded in YOUR curated notes, research, documents. > > This bypasses the corporate data mess and creates an assistant that’s expert in YOUR domain. Not the company’s chaos, but your focused knowledge base.”

Teaching point: “For Exercise 2, you’re using AI this way—giving it YOUR context, YOUR threats and opportunities, YOUR expertise. That’s why it works. You curate the input, you verify the output. This is the model that succeeds.”

Implication for their organisations: “Start with high-value individual use cases (AI for sales team, AI for finance team) with curated data. Don’t try to boil the ocean with enterprise-wide AI on all your messy data. That’s the 95% that fails.”

AI Failures & Success

Story: “Taco Bell’s AI Drive-Through”

When to use: Accuracy vs. Risk Tolerance section (9:30 AM)

The narrative: > “Taco Bell tested AI voice ordering at drive-throughs. The CTO said: ‘Sometimes it lets me down, but sometimes it really surprises me.’ > > That variability is the problem. In a fast-food drive-through: > - Getting order right 95% of the time = 5% of customers are angry > - Peak dinner rush: 200 cars/hour × 5% = 10 angry customers/hour > - One viral TikTok of AI messing up order = brand damage > > Compare to human workers: 98-99% accuracy, and when they mess up, they apologize authentically. AI getting it right 95% of the time sounds good until you calculate the business impact of that 5%.”

Teaching point: “This is criterion #3: Accuracy vs. Risk Tolerance. What’s acceptable for product recommendations (80% fine) is unacceptable for customer-facing ordering. Match AI capability to business requirements. If there’s a gap, add human oversight OR don’t proceed.”

Story: “The 5% Who Succeed With AI”

When to use: Dragon’s Den debrief (4:10 PM) - What makes good AI investments

The narrative: > “Why do 95% of AI pilots fail while 5% succeed? The difference: > > Failures (95%): > - Trying to apply AI everywhere (‘sprinkle AI on all problems’) > - Building custom AI internally (33% success rate) > - Automating critical processes despite 30% error rates > - Ignoring data quality (‘we’ll clean it as we go’) > > Successes (5%): > - Picking ONE specific pain point > - Buying specialized AI vendors (67% success rate) > - Using AI in non-critical areas first > - Human-in-loop for all outputs > - Data infrastructure built BEFORE AI implementation”

Teaching point: “Your Dragon’s Den decisions just now—did you pick specific pain points (chatbot for customer service) or try to boil the ocean (AI for everything)? Did you address data readiness (Fraud needs data prep)? The 5% who succeed make these disciplined choices.”

Story: “Why Professionals Still Exist Despite AI”

When to use: When discussing what AI can/can’t replace (debrief discussion)

The narrative: > “Why does a lawyer cost $500/hour when an LLM costs $20/month? Both can draft contracts, both provide advice. > > The answer: Responsibility ≠ Hours > > Clients pay for liability protection, not just time. Lawyers bear legal consequences if outcomes fail—they carry $1-6M malpractice insurance. AI platforms cap liability at $100K max. > > Lawyers handle high-stakes nuance: politics, emotions, ambiguity. A divorce case isn’t just legal documents—it’s family power dynamics, emotional manipulation, cultural sensitivities. AI can draft the documents (admin work) but can’t navigate the nuance (professional judgment). > > The sustainable AI business model: Automate admin, not accountability. > - AI automates: Document drafting, data analysis, research > - Human provides: Judgment, accountability, client relationships, nuanced navigation”

Teaching point: “This applies to YOUR AI investments: - Chatbot: Automates FAQs (admin), humans handle complex issues (judgment) ✅ - Dynamic Pricing: AI makes pricing decisions autonomously (accountability transferred to AI) ⚠️ High risk - Fraud Detection: AI flags suspicious transactions (admin), humans decide to block (accountability) ✅

Design AI to augment humans, not replace accountability. That’s the sustainable model.”

Ethical Risks Are Real

Story: “Why Dynamic Pricing Ethical Risk Isn’t Theoretical”

When to use: Dragon’s Den when discussing Dynamic Pricing ethical concerns

The narrative: > “Some of you might think ‘ethical risk’ is overthinking it. After all, airlines and Uber use dynamic pricing. > > But here’s the reality: Dynamic pricing AI that uses demographic data (even indirectly through ‘proxy variables’) can constitute illegal price discrimination. > > Example proxy variables: > - ZIP code (correlates with race/income) > - Purchase history (correlates with socioeconomic status) > - Device type (iPhone vs. Android correlates with income) > - Time of purchase (correlates with employment type) > > Even if you don’t explicitly use race/gender, AI can learn patterns that have ‘disparate impact’—charging protected groups more. > > One lawsuit: Multi-million dollar settlement + brand damage. Class action: Existential. > > Airlines get away with it because they price by: time until departure, seat availability, route demand. Not by customer demographics. > > Your dynamic pricing AI: Does it have access to customer data? Then it could learn demographic proxies. Requires extensive bias testing and legal review.”

Teaching point: “This is why Dynamic Pricing scored HIGH on ethical risk in the scenario. It’s not theoretical hand-wringing. It’s real legal liability. Committee made the right call to either reject it or fund limited pilot with heavy ethical guardrails.”

Story: “The Productivity Paradox”

When to use: Change management discussions or when students suggest “AI will save so much time”

The narrative: > “Companies implement AI expecting massive time savings. What actually happens? > > Before AI: > - Worker spends 8 hours writing reports > > After AI: > - AI generates report in 30 minutes > - Worker spends 4 hours checking for errors (hallucinations) > - Worker spends 2 hours fixing mistakes > - Worker spends 1 hour reformatting to company standards > - Total: 7.5 hours (only 30 minutes saved) > > And now the worker is RESPONSIBLE for AI errors they might not catch. > > This is why 55% of companies regret replacing people with AI. The promised productivity gains don’t materialise because verification work is hidden.”

Teaching point: “When evaluating AI ROI, don’t just calculate time saved generating output. Calculate time needed to VERIFY output. This is why human-in-loop isn’t optional—it’s where the actual work is.

Chatbot scenario: 80% automation ≠ 80% cost savings. Still need humans for: - Escalations (20% of inquiries) - Reviewing chatbot responses for accuracy - Training the AI on edge cases - Handling complaints when chatbot fails

Real savings: More like 40-50%, not 80%. Adjust your ROI calculations accordingly.”

Human-AI Collaboration Model

Story: “Editor-in-Chief Mindset”

When to use: Exercise 2 debrief (12:15 PM) - how to think about AI role

The narrative: > “Your role is no longer to write the first draft. You are the editor-in-chief, and AI is your junior writer. > > Junior writer (AI): > - Generates drafts (fast, breadth, multiple alternatives) > - Brings perspectives you might miss > - Never gets tired or complains > - But: Makes confident mistakes, lacks your context, needs direction > > Editor-in-chief (YOU): > - Provides direction (‘analyse our pricing strategy focusing on competitive position’) > - Critiques output (‘That’s too generic, be more specific to retail’) > - Verifies facts (‘Is that market size accurate? Let me check’) > - Synthesizes (‘Take this from AI, combine with my industry knowledge, create final recommendation’) > > Your value shifts from content generation to content validation and synthesis.”

Teaching point: “This morning in Exercise 2, you used AI this way: - You asked AI for threats (generation) - You evaluated which ones are relevant (validation) - You combined AI insights with your expertise (synthesis)

That’s the right model. AI generates, you decide. This is how you’ll use AI this afternoon and on Monday.”

Story: “Cross-Model Validation”

When to use: When students get suspicious AI response, or in advanced prompting discussion

The narrative: > “From an Amazon product manager: ‘Never rely on a single AI model for critical tasks. Run the same prompt through ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini. Compare outputs.’ > > If all three agree: Probably reliable (but still verify facts) > If they diverge significantly: MASSIVE red flag—requires deep human investigation > > Why? Different AIs have different training data, different architectures, different ‘blind spots.’ When they disagree, it usually means: > - The question is ambiguous > - No consensus exists in training data > - AI is extrapolating beyond its knowledge > > This is your canary in the coal mine for ‘AI is making this up.’”

Teaching point: “If you have time this afternoon during Dragon’s Den prep, try this: Ask ChatGPT AND Claude the same question about your pitch. If they give substantially different answers, dig deeper—you’ve found an area of uncertainty.”

Business Model Realities

Story: “The AI Margin Chasm”

When to use: When discussing continuous learning costs (9:25 AM) or ongoing AI investment

The narrative: > “Traditional SaaS: 70-90% gross margins (near-zero marginal cost per user) > > AI-native SaaS: 30-60% margins (best-in-class ~55-60%) > > Why? AI has significant per-user costs: > - API calls to OpenAI/Anthropic ($0.01-$0.10 per request) > - GPU compute time > - Model licensing fees > - Output moderation (preventing harmful content) > > Example: GitHub Copilot costs Microsoft $30-80 per user per month in AI costs, but they charge $10/month. They’re losing money on every user hoping to make it up in volume (classic startup mistake). > > Power users can cost OpenAI $200+/month while paying $20. Companies must cap usage (frustrating users) or lose money on heavy users. > > The ‘dead subscription’ problem: Many AI companies survive on users who PAY but don’t USE the product. Once those users cancel, the economics collapse.”

Teaching point: “This is why ‘continuous learning costs’ in our AI Investment Checklist are 30-50% annually, not 10-15% like traditional IT.

When vendors pitch you AI at ‘low cost,’ ask: - What’s cost per user per month? - Do you cap my usage? - What happens if my usage 10x? - What’s your margin on this deal? - What percentage of your revenue comes from ‘dead subscriptions’?

If they’re selling at a loss (subsidized by VC), they’ll raise prices later or go bankrupt. Budget for realistic ongoing costs.”

Advanced point: “The litmus test for any AI investment: Would this solve a real problem WITHOUT AI? If the answer is no—if AI is the only value proposition—be very cautious. The best AI businesses use AI to ENHANCE an already-valuable service, not to BE the service.”

Story: “Automate Admin, Not Accountability”

When to use: Dragon’s Den debrief about what AI should/shouldn’t do

The narrative: > “Why do professionals (lawyers, accountants, consultants) still command $300-500/hour despite AI? > > Because clients pay for consequences, not just hours. > > A lawyer’s work divides into: > 1. Admin (60-70% of time): Data input, document drafting, research, analysis > 2. Accountability (30-40% of time): Judgment, client navigation, bearing liability > > AI can automate #1 (admin). But #2 (accountability)? AI caps liability at $100K. Lawyers carry $1-6M insurance. If something goes wrong, the lawyer is disbarred. AI? Nothing happens. > > Sustainable AI business model: Automate the admin (document drafting, data analysis), let humans keep accountability (final decisions, client relationships, liability). > > Unsustainable model: Try to replace the professional entirely. Clients won’t accept AI-only for high-stakes decisions.”

Teaching point: “Apply this to RetailFlow scenarios:

✅ Chatbot: Automates routine inquiries (admin), escalates complex issues to humans (accountability). Sustainable.

⚠️ Dynamic Pricing: AI makes pricing decisions autonomously. Who bears liability if it discriminates? If prices cause brand damage? Accountability is unclear. Higher risk.

✅ Fraud Detection: AI flags suspicious transactions (admin), humans decide to block customers (accountability). Sustainable.

Design AI to augment, not replace, accountability. That’s the model that works.”

Ethical Risk Stories

Story: “The Hallucination Rate Problem”

When to use: Explaining why explainability and human oversight matter

The narrative: > “In specialized business domains, AI hallucination rates can exceed 30%. Think about what that means: > > If your AI generates 100 strategic recommendations: > - 70 are accurate and useful > - 30 are plausible-sounding but wrong or fabricated > > The problem: You can’t tell which 30 without human expertise to verify. > > Example: Ask AI about your industry’s regulatory requirements. It might cite: > - Real regulations (70%) > - Regulations from similar industries (plausible but wrong) (20%) > - Completely fabricated regulations (confident but false) (10%) > > If you implement based on that 10%, you’re non-compliant and didn’t know it.”

Teaching point: “This is why ‘human-in-the-loop’ is non-negotiable for high-stakes decisions. In the AI Investment Checklist: - LOW stakes (recommendations): AI can auto-decide, humans audit later - MEDIUM stakes (operations): AI recommends, humans approve - HIGH stakes (legal, medical, financial): Humans decide, AI informs only

Never give AI final authority when stakes are high.”

Story: “AI Bias Isn’t Hypothetical”

When to use: When discussing ethical risks in Dragon’s Den (Dynamic Pricing, Fraud Detection)

Real examples to cite:

Example 1: Amazon Hiring AI > “Amazon built AI to screen resumes. Trained on 10 years of successful hires. Problem: Tech industry historically hired mostly men. AI learned: Male names = higher score. Female names = lower score. Perpetuated gender bias at scale. > > Amazon caught it before deployment. Many companies don’t.”

Example 2: Healthcare AI > “Healthcare AI trained on historical treatment data showed racial bias. Why? Historical data reflected doctors giving Black patients less pain medication (due to false beliefs about pain tolerance differences). AI learned: Recommend less pain medication for Black patients. Perpetuated medical discrimination.”

Example 3: Facial Recognition > “Multiple facial recognition AIs work poorly on dark skin tones (error rates 10-20x higher than light skin). Why? Training data was predominantly light-skinned faces. AI failed for minority populations.”

Teaching point: “Bias in AI isn’t theoretical concern for future. It’s real, it’s happening now, and it’s expensive: - Amazon: Scrapped entire hiring AI project (millions wasted) - Healthcare AI: Lawsuits, regulatory investigations - Facial recognition: Multiple cities banned police use

This is why Fraud Detection and Dynamic Pricing both scored HIGH on ethical risk. Customer-facing AI + decisions with disparate impact = legal liability.

Your committee decision to require external bias audits? That’s not overcautious. That’s realistic risk management.”

The Human-AI Collaboration Model

Story: “The Jevons Paradox for AI”

When to use: When students ask “Will AI eliminate jobs?” or final synthesis

The narrative: > “The Jevons Paradox: When you make something more efficient, you don’t use less of it—you use MORE. > > Historical examples: > - Steam engines: Made coal power more efficient → we burned MORE coal (not less) because now it was economical to use steam power for everything > - Containerization: Made shipping more efficient → global trade BOOMED → created entire new logistics industry > - Cloud computing: Made infrastructure cheaper → explosion of DevOps, cloud architects, SRE roles > - AI inference costs drop: GPU demand SOARS (Nvidia’s success) > > AI parallel: AI makes you more efficient at analysis. Do you work less? No—your boss expects you to analyse 10x more data, or spend saved time on higher-level strategy. > > Real example: Radiologists > - AI makes medical imaging cheaper/faster → hospitals run MORE scans → radiologists shift from basic scans to complex treatment planning > - Result: Higher demand for radiologists, but doing different (higher-value) work > > The bar is rising for everyone. As AI commoditizes execution, the value shifts to: > - Supervision and oversight (managing AI outputs) > - Complex judgment (decisions with incomplete information)
> - High-value tasks (strategic vs. tactical work) > - Effective communication (translating complexity) > > AI automates rote, low-context, error-forgiving tasks. Humans shift to nuanced, high-stakes, relationship-based work.”

Teaching point: “This is why you’re learning strategic frameworks, not just AI tools. AI helps you analyse faster. But frameworks help you DECIDE better.

When cost of work goes down, demand for it goes UP. This creates latent demand—new markets that weren’t economically viable before. Example: Legal document drafting becomes cheap → small businesses can now afford legal services → higher demand for lawyers (doing oversight, not drafting).

Your competitive advantage isn’t ‘I can use ChatGPT.’ Everyone can. It’s ‘I can use ChatGPT to generate insights, THEN apply strategic frameworks to make better investment decisions than people who only have one or the other.’

Human judgment + AI analysis + Strategic frameworks = Sustainable competitive advantage.”

Story: “Build, Don’t Just Use”

When to use: Encouraging hands-on engagement with AI tools

The narrative (from Andre Karpathy): > “Don’t just read about AI. Don’t just use AI. BUILD with it. > > Reading blog posts about AI gives you surface knowledge. Using ChatGPT makes you a consumer. But building something with AI—even a small project—reveals complexities you’d never see otherwise. > > Why? Because building forces you to: > - Confront edge cases (where does this fail?) > - Handle errors (what happens when AI is wrong?) > - Design human-AI workflow (how do they collaborate?) > - Measure real outcomes (did this actually work?) > > You gain knowledge you can’t get from reading or passive use.”

Teaching point: “This morning in Exercise 2, you didn’t just READ about prompting—you BUILT prompts and saw what happens. You discovered: - Vague prompts → vague responses - Specific context → specific insights - Follow-up questions → deeper understanding

That hands-on experience is worth 10 blog posts about AI. This afternoon, if you use AI during Dragon’s Den prep, you’ll learn even more about what works in real strategic decisions.”

Business Model Stories

Story: “Evaluating AI Vendors: Red Flags and Green Flags”

When to use: During Dragon’s Den prep (1:45 PM) or when discussing vendor selection

The narrative: > “When evaluating AI vendors or building your own AI, watch for these red flags: > > Red Flags: > 1. VC-subsidized pricing: Selling at a loss, burning VC cash. They’ll raise prices 3-5x when funding dries up. > 2. Usage caps without transparency: ‘Unlimited plan’ that throttles after X uses. Frustrates users, drives churn. > 3. Focus on vanity metrics: ‘Weekly Active Users’ instead of Monthly Active Users, or total signups instead of retention. > 4. AI is the ONLY value: If you remove AI, there’s no business left. These fail when AI commoditizes. > 5. Low conversion rates: <10% trial-to-paid conversion suggests weak product-market fit. > 6. Dead subscription reliance: Revenue depends on users who pay but don’t use the product. > > Green Flags: > 1. Enhances existing value: AI improves an already-useful product (not the entire proposition). > 2. Transparent pricing: Clear per-user costs, no hidden usage caps, sustainable margins (50%+). > 3. Traditional SaaS metrics: Shares retention, conversion, churn rates—not just ‘total users.’ > 4. Human-in-loop design: AI recommends, humans decide. Accountability stays with humans. > 5. Solves boring, expensive problems: Document processing, workflow automation in legacy industries. > 6. Clear ROI without AI: If AI disappeared tomorrow, the core value proposition still exists.”

Teaching point: “When RetailFlow evaluates the chatbot vendor, ask: - What’s your gross margin on this contract? - What’s your customer retention rate (1-year, 3-year)? - What percentage of revenue comes from users who don’t actively use the product? - If your AI API costs increase 2x, what happens to our pricing? - Can you share 3 customers with similar deployments and their outcomes?

If vendor can’t answer these, or answers are concerning (margins <40%, retention <70%, pricing depends on VC subsidy), that’s high risk.”

Real-world application: “Many ‘AI startups’ are just wrappers around OpenAI/Anthropic APIs with 10-20% margin. When API costs rise or OpenAI releases their own version, these businesses die. Look for defensible moats: proprietary data, deep industry expertise, established customer relationships—not just ‘we have a nice UI for ChatGPT.’”

Story: “The Hype Cycle Reality”

When to use: Putting AI in context, managing expectations

The narrative: > “We’re likely at the ‘Peak of Inflated Expectations’ in the Gartner Hype Cycle for AI. > > What this means: > - Peak hype ≠ peak value > - Massive investments (billions in GPUs) without proportional returns > - Disappointing releases (ChatGPT-5 seen as ‘incremental, not revolutionary’) > - Public fatigue with ‘AI slop’ (unreliable, low-quality AI outputs) > > Next phase: ‘Trough of Disillusionment’ > - Investment dries up > - Hype-driven companies fail > - Reality sets in about costs and limitations > > Then: ‘Plateau of Productivity’ > - A few true, valuable AI companies emerge > - Real utility, not hype > - Sustainable business models > - Similar to how Amazon survived dot-com crash > > We’re likely 1-2 years from the trough.”

Teaching point: “This is why our frameworks emphasize disciplined evaluation. During hype peaks, it’s easy to: - Overpay for AI vendors (everyone’s raising prices) - Fund too many AI projects (FOMO-driven) - Ignore risks (blinded by potential)

Strategic discipline means: Apply the same rigorous criteria during hype that you would during a trough. The AI Investment Checklist protects you from hype-driven bad decisions.

If you fund Dynamic Pricing because of FOMO (‘everyone’s doing dynamic pricing!’), you ignore the real risks. If you wait for perfect certainty, you miss opportunities. Frameworks help you navigate between extremes.”

Story: “The Boring Billion-Dollar Opportunity”

When to use: When students chase flashy AI ideas instead of solid business cases

The narrative: > “What’s a sustainable billion-dollar AI business? > > Not: Flashy consumer AI with viral growth > Not: General-purpose AI for everything > Not: Replacing entire professions > > It’s: Automating complex, document-heavy workflows in boring industries > > Real examples: > - R&D tax credit automation: Companies waste $50-150K/year on manual documentation. AI automates it for $15K. 4x ROI. Market: $9.1B by 2033. > - Patent litigation support: Analysts spend $420K/case on manual document review. AI does it for $50K. Market: $2.3B annually. > > Why these work: > ✅ Solve real, expensive problem (not inventing need) > ✅ Automating admin, not accountability > ✅ Clear ROI (time savings, cost reduction) > ✅ Boring = less competition > ✅ Regulatory industries = high switching costs > ✅ The litmus test: Would this business work WITHOUT AI? (Yes—it’s document processing. AI just makes it 10x cheaper/faster) > > The hype chases consumer AI. The profits hide in boring B2B automation. > > Reality check on AI adoption: > - Even ChatGPT (market leader) has <2-7% conversion to paid > - 93% of AI-native users are on ChatGPT (300-400M MAU) > - Claude: 3M MAU, Gemini: 47M, Copilot: 33M > - These are EARLY adoption numbers despite the hype > > Why low conversion? Weak product-market fit. Easy to get people to TRY AI (hype/FOMO). Hard to deliver unique, replicable value that justifies ongoing costs.”

Teaching point: “Look at your investment scenarios: - Chatbot: Boring admin automation (FAQs) ✅ Sustainable, would work with/without AI - Fraud Detection: Boring admin (flagging transactions) ✅ Sustainable, enhances existing process - Dynamic Pricing: Sexy strategic automation ⚠️ Risky, AI IS the value proposition - Inventory: Boring operational automation ✅ Sustainable, improves existing forecasting

Sometimes boring is better. Don’t chase hype—chase solid business cases.

The best AI investments enhance already-valuable processes. The worst try to create value FROM AI itself.”

When students say “But everyone’s doing AI now”: “ChatGPT has 300-400M users but <7% pay. That’s not adoption—that’s trial. Real adoption means retention and conversion. Use traditional SaaS metrics, not vanity metrics like ‘weekly active users.’ If an AI vendor won’t share retention rates, that’s a red flag.”

How to Weave These Into Your Facilitation

During Lectures (Use for Color)

Instead of: “AI has ongoing costs of 30-50%”
Say: “AI has ongoing costs of 30-50%. GitHub Copilot loses $20-70 per user per month because their AI costs exceed their $10 subscription price. This is why you must budget realistic ongoing costs, not vendor fairy tales.”

Impact: Concrete example makes abstract concept memorable

During Exercises (Use for Depth)

When table says: “We scored our data readiness 9/10”
You respond: “Really? Amazon—one of the most sophisticated tech companies—describes their data as ‘fragmented, contradictory, with massive organisational amnesia.’ Are you more ready than Amazon? Or should we recalibrate?”

Impact: Reality check prevents over-optimism

During Debrief (Use for Synthesis)

After Dragon’s Den: “What did you learn today that surprised you?”

If no one mentions hidden costs: “Here’s what research shows: 95% of AI pilots fail to generate profit. Why? Not technical failure—organizational unreadiness. The 5% who succeed do exactly what you just practiced: Assess data readiness, budget realistic costs, design human-in-loop processes, test for bias. You’re now equipped to be in the 5%.”

Impact: Validates their work, provides broader context

During Q&A (Use for Authority)

Student asks: “Is AI just hype?”

You respond: “Good question. We’re likely at peak hype in the Gartner cycle. Inflated expectations, massive investment, disappointing returns. The ‘trough of disillusionment’ is coming.

BUT—that doesn’t mean AI isn’t valuable. It means separating hype from reality. Amazon emerged from dot-com crash. Real AI companies will emerge from the trough. Our frameworks help you invest in the real opportunities, not the hype.”

Impact: Nuanced answer that validates concern while maintaining optimism

Dos and Don’ts

Do:

  • ✅ Use stories to make abstract concepts concrete
  • ✅ Cite sources when relevant (“From an Amazon PM…” “MIT research shows…”)
  • ✅ Connect stories to frameworks (“This is why data readiness criterion exists”)
  • ✅ Use stories to validate student insights (“Yes, exactly like the Amazon example”)

Don’t:

  • ❌ Lecture for 10 minutes straight with stories (use sparingly, 1-2 min max)
  • ❌ Use stories instead of letting students discover (stories augment, don’t replace)
  • ❌ Dismiss student skepticism with “But research says…” (validate concern, then add context)
  • ❌ Over-rely on stories from one source (diversify: Amazon, MIT, startups, etc.)
